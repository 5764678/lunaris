## Q1. What existing tools offer for teaching the Moon at secondary level, and what each one lacks (feature-by-feature comparison)

### Takeaway
No tool found, Thai or international, lets a secondary student stand on the real lunar south-pole terrain, see Sun and Earth paths over the true local horizon, and then make a transparent, evidence-labelled landing-site decision. International tools have real data but are English-only, built for experts or passive viewing, and not tied to Thai indicators. Thai tools (IPST, NARIT, DLTV, TruePlookpanya) are in Thai and follow the curriculum, but they show only the Earth-based view of phases and use no mission data. LUNARIS's niche is where those two groups do not overlap. To claim it, LUNARIS still needs the things only the Thai tools have today: a Thai interface, tasks mapped to the indicators, a teacher guide, and a mode that runs on low-end devices.

### Inferences
- **The gap, stated explicitly:** no free, Thai-language, curriculum-mapped tool lets secondary students (1) take the Moon's point of view on real terrain, (2) explain from evidence why the south pole has near-continuous sunlight on high ridges and permanently shadowed craters that can trap water ice, (3) find when Earth is visible for communications, and (4) make and defend a landing-site decision with visible trade-offs and data provenance. Moon Trek and QuickMap have the data but no pedagogy. NAAP, SVS and the Thai tools have the pedagogy but stay in the Earth observer's frame and use no mission data.
- **Second differentiator: evidence and provenance literacy.** None of the tools reviewed labels where each number comes from or how certain it is. That fits Thai science-process and media-literacy goals, and judges can see it at a glance on the poster.
- **Third differentiator: reasoning about decisions.** Pareto dominance with user-set weights turns astronomy into a STEM engineering-design task that combines science, maths and technology. Thai tools do not do this.
- **Weaknesses to fix before 27 Nov:** English-only interface, no Thai indicator mapping, no teacher guide or lesson plan, and unknown performance on low-end Android phones and school PCs. These are exactly the strengths of IPST, DLTV and NARIT.
- **Positioning:** present LUNARIS as complementary. Students first learn phases from the Earth-based view (NAAP, IPST, NARIT), then switch to the Moon's view in LUNARIS for space-technology and Artemis-era exploration indicators. This avoids competing head-on with IPST.
- **Curriculum hook [K, unverified]:** I believe the revised 2560 indicators include ว 3.1 ม.3/3 (phases, Moon rise/set times, tides) and ว 3.1 ม.3/4 (uses of space technology and progress of space-exploration programmes), plus the upper-secondary additional course in Earth, astronomy and space. Check the exact wording against the IPST indicator book before citing it.

### Inferences
- The 2569 cycle looks like the template for 2570: a December submission window, a short turnaround to results, and a ceremony tied to Teacher's Day. The current timeline (deadline 27 Nov 2026, shortlist 11 Dec, finals 14 Jan 2027, awards 15 Jan 2027) fits that pattern but runs about 10 days earlier. The 2570 student categories appear to be new, so there may be no past student winners to benchmark.
- **What the judging criteria imply** (creativity and problem-solving design; appropriate technology; practical use and commercial potential; presentation):
  - *Problem-solving design:* frame a Thai classroom problem first. Moon phases and space topics are abstract and taught only from the Earth view; students lack real-data and decision-making practice. Then show the design response.
  - *Appropriate technology:* judges in Thai education contests usually value fit to context over sophistication. Show that it runs in a phone browser, works offline or on low bandwidth, and costs nothing. A heavy 3D app could count against it.
  - *Practical use:* the 2569 call was for "นวัตกรรมทางการศึกษา", which in Thai practice usually implies classroom trial evidence such as pre/post tests and satisfaction surveys. Even a small pilot with one class at a partner school before 27 Nov would strengthen the entry. This is an inference about the norm, not the published rubric.
  - *Commercial potential:* a clear path to scale, such as the DEEP platform, NARIT or IPST partnership, or district training (see Q3).
- Satit Chula's track record suggests finalists are polished, prototype-plus-evidence entries that also travel to invention expos. Entries that read as research software dumps would likely lose to these. The poster and 3-minute video should foreground one student task, such as "choose a landing site for Thailand's first lunar payload".

## Q3. Realistic sustainability and commercial models for a Thai EdTech product

### Takeaway
Thai K-12 edtech has scaled through three channels: free-to-student consumer apps funded by paid add-ons (StartDee); B2B and government deployments to school networks (Edsy, reportedly with BMA schools); and government platforms that host private-sector content (DEEP). For LUNARIS, the realistic model is open or free core access for schools, with a light hosting footprint. Revenue or sustainability would come from teacher training and workshops, partner-funded deployment (NARIT, IPST, MHESI programmes, provincial or BMA networks), and listing on DEEP. Selling subscriptions to schools for a single-topic tool is unlikely to work.

### Inferences
Realistic models for LUNARIS, from most to least realistic:
1. **Free core plus training and certification (recommended).** The web app stays free for Thai schools. Income comes from paid or sponsored teacher workshops; a "space-mission STEM" short course could count toward teacher professional-development hours (possibly via the Faculty of Education, Chulalongkorn, or partner universities); and school camps. The Thai Satit / demonstration-school network is a natural first channel.
2. **Institutional partnership and grants.** Pitch to NARIT (an add-on module for its telescope-distribution schools), IPST (a supplementary digital medium for the space-technology content), MHESI and NRCT youth-innovation funds, and listing on DEEP. This gives national reach at zero marginal cost to schools, which suits public schools' procurement limits.
3. **District, provincial or BMA licensing of a "classroom edition".** Possible features are a teacher dashboard, class codes and printable Thai worksheets. Edsy shows BMA-scale deployments happen (unverified figures), but procurement is slow. Treat this as a year-2+ option.
4. **Open-source with a services model.** The NASA data sources are public, and an open-source core builds trust with judges and IPST. Revenue would come from customization, for example adding other sites, Artemis updates, or a Thai satellite-mission module.
5. **Freemium consumer app.** Weak fit: single-topic depth, and Thai consumer edtech is dominated by tutoring-style apps (StartDee etc.).
- **Hosting cost [K, not verified]:** if LUNARIS ships precomputed SPICE and LOLA products as static files, it can run on free or low-cost static hosting with a CDN for about USD 0–20 per month at school scale. This keeps the "zero cost to schools" claim credible for the "commercial potential" criterion. Confirm actual asset sizes and egress before quoting a number.
- For the entry form, state the sustainability model as a phased plan: Phase 1 is a pilot with Satit Chula and partner schools; Phase 2 is a NARIT/IPST partnership and DEEP listing; Phase 3 is a training and certification business plus a Thai lunar-mission content extension.
