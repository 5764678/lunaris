## Q1. PISA: Thailand's science, mathematics and reading results (2022, plus the newly released PISA 2025)

### Takeaway
In PISA 2022, Thailand scored 409 in science, 394 in mathematics and 379 in reading, far below the OECD averages of 485, 472 and 476. Only 47% of Thai students reached baseline Level 2 in science (OECD: 76%), and only 32% did so in mathematics (OECD: 69%). **Contrary to the brief's assumption, PISA 2025 results are already out: the OECD released them on 8 Sept 2026.** Thailand improved to 432 in science, 407 in mathematics and 392 in reading, but it is still below the OECD average in every domain.

### Cited Findings
**PISA 2022 (Thai national centre, IPST, and OECD-derived figures)**
- Thailand PISA 2022: mathematics 394 (−25 vs 2018), science 409 (−17 vs 2018), reading 379 (−14 vs 2018). OECD averages are 472 (mathematics), 485 (science) and 476 (reading). — [IPST press release, 6 Dec 2023](https://www.ipst.ac.th/news/54912/20231106-pisa2022.html)
  - Implied 2018 scores: science 426, mathematics 419, reading 393. The 419 and 393 match UN/UNICEF. — [UN Thailand](https://thailand.un.org/en/node/314239)
- Share of students at **Level 2 or above** (baseline proficiency):
  - Mathematics: Thailand 32%, OECD 69%
  - Science: Thailand 47%, OECD 76%
  - Reading: Thailand 35%, OECD 74%
  - This means roughly **68% (mathematics), 53% (science) and 65% (reading)** of Thai 15-year-olds are below baseline (my arithmetic from the IPST figures). — [IPST](https://www.ipst.ac.th/news/54912/20231106-pisa2022.html); maths 32% vs 69% also confirmed by [UNICEF Thailand, Jan 2026](https://www.unicef.org/thailand/stories/fix-thailands-education-its-too-late)
- Sample: 8,495 students from 279 schools in all school affiliations, tested in August 2022 (computer-based). — [IPST](https://www.ipst.ac.th/news/54912/20231106-pisa2022.html)
- Since PISA 2000, Thailand's mathematics and reading show a downward trend. Science shows no statistically significant change. — [IPST](https://www.ipst.ac.th/news/54912/20231106-pisa2022.html)
- From 2012 to 2022, scores fell by about 30 points in mathematics and science and about 60 points in reading. — [UNICEF Thailand](https://www.unicef.org/thailand/stories/fix-thailands-education-its-too-late)
- Only 1% of Thai students are top performers in mathematics, against 9% across the OECD. — [UNICEF Thailand](https://www.unicef.org/thailand/stories/fix-thailands-education-its-too-late)
- The Equitable Education Fund (EEF/กสศ.) described PISA 2022 as Thailand's lowest result in 20 years. — [EEF analysis](https://www.eef.or.th/article-pisa-2022-00/) (cited in search results; full page not fetched)

**PISA 2025 (released 8 Sept 2026)**
- Thailand PISA 2025, with OECD averages in brackets:
  - Science 432 (OECD 482)
  - Reading 392 (OECD 461)
  - Mathematics 407 (OECD 463)
  - Computational problem-solving 476 (OECD 500)
  - Changes vs 2022: science +23, reading +13, mathematics +13. — [The Nation, 10 Sept 2026](https://www.nationthailand.com/news/policy/40070867)
- **36.9%** of Thai students were below Level 2 in science, reading *and* mathematics at the same time. Only **1.2%** reached Level 5 or 6 in at least one subject. — [The Nation](https://www.nationthailand.com/news/policy/40070867)
- Thailand ranked about 53rd of 91 in science, up from 58th in 2022. — [Pattaya Mail](https://www.pattayamail.com/thailandnews/thailand-rises-to-53rd-in-pisa-2025-as-scores-improve-across-all-subjects-564536) (from search results only; I did not open the article)
- Other Thai PISA 2025 indicators relevant to LUNARIS:
  - Growth mindset: 36.3% (OECD 69.3%)
  - Students in schools reporting **no teacher shortage**: only 46.6%
  - Students in schools reporting **no shortage of learning materials**: only 36.8%
  - Students who use AI at least weekly: 50.7% (OECD 45.5%) — [The Nation](https://www.nationthailand.com/news/policy/40070867)
- OECD Thailand country note for PISA 2025 (the URL exists): [OECD PISA 2025 Country Note: Thailand](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pisa-2025-results-volume-i-country-notes_2d4ff9ea-en/thailand_dfc5bcb9-en.html). It returned HTTP 403 to my fetch, so I could not read it. A search summary of it said "PISA sampling standards were not met" for Thailand, but the data were judged "fit for reporting". This is **unverified**.

### Inferences
- In the proposal, use PISA 2025 as the headline (most recent). Use PISA 2022 for the baseline and proficiency-level detail. Even after the 2025 rebound, Thai science is ~50 points (about half a standard deviation) below the OECD mean. In 2022, about half of students lacked baseline science literacy.
- PISA 2025 made science the **major domain**, so OECD Volume I should report competency subscales (e.g., "interpret data and evidence scientifically"). These would be the strongest evidence for LUNARIS's data-interpretation angle, and someone should pull them directly from the OECD report or PISA Data Explorer.
- The PISA 2025 figures for growth mindset and for teacher and material shortages support a free, browser-based tool that needs no special equipment.

### Gaps
- **Sub-competency data**: I found no Thailand-specific figure for "interpret data and evidence scientifically" in PISA 2022. Science was a *minor* domain in 2022, so the OECD did not report competency subscales then. PISA 2025 subscales likely exist, but I could not access the OECD country note (403). Get them from OECD's PISA 2025 Volume I or the IPST PISA Thailand site.
- PISA 2025 share of Thai students below Level 2 *in science alone*: not found. Only the combined 36.9% (below Level 2 in all three) was found.
- PISA 2015 Thai scores (science 421, as I recall): **not verified in this session**.
- A search summary claimed that Thailand's 2025 science "remained below 2015 and 2018 levels". This contradicts the scores (432 vs ~421/426), so I treat the claim as garbled and unreliable.

## Q4. Thai research on astronomy misconceptions (มโนทัศน์/มโนมติที่คลาดเคลื่อน): Moon phases, eclipses, tides, seasons

### Takeaway
Thai studies are few and small-sample, but consistent. Thai lower-secondary students and Thai pre-service science teachers mostly hold incomplete or wrong models of the Sun–Earth–Moon system. **"Earth's shadow causes Moon phases" is the dominant misconception in Thailand**, just as it is internationally. Thai students also link tides to the Moon "rising at night" and believe seasons come from Earth–Sun distance.

### Cited Findings
**Thai pre-service science teachers (Khon Kaen University)**
- Saenpuk & Ruangsuwan (KKU, Faculty of Education and Faculty of Science) studied **26 third- and fourth-year pre-service science teachers** in an earth science/astronomy methods course (semester 1, 2016), using open-ended questions on the cause of Moon phases. — [Saenpuk & Ruangsuwan, "The pre-service teachers understanding about moon phase" (conference paper, IOP J. Phys. Conf. Ser. format)](https://indico.global/event/8304/papers/76194/files/1891-98.The_pre-service_teachers_understanding_about_moon_phase.pdf)
  - Only **2 of 26 (7.7%)** showed sound understanding. Most showed partial understanding. 6 had partial understanding plus a specific misconception, and 3 had a specific misconception. (The text elsewhere says 2 in the misconception category, an internal inconsistency in the paper.)
  - Only **3 of 26 (11.5%)** referred to all three bodies (Sun, Earth and Moon) when explaining phases. **18 of 26 (69.2%)** used only the Earth–Moon relationship.
  - The misconceptions quoted are the Earth's shadow type, e.g., "The Sun, Moon and Earth are stand in a row. And the shadow of the earth falls on the moon."
  - Publication year was not confirmed (data collected in 2016).

**Thai lower-secondary students (Sisaket)**
- Jindawong (2555/2012), "The Effect of Instructional Activities Promoting Scientific Reasoning on Lower Secondary School Students' Mental Representation of Basic Astronomy Phenomena". Sample: **28 lower-secondary students** at โรงเรียนเฉลิมพระเกียรติสมเด็จพระศรีนครินทร์ ศรีสะเกษ (volunteers, after-school). Four concepts: seasons, solar eclipse, Moon phases, tides. — [thaiedresearch.org PDF](http://backoffice.thaiedresearch.org/uploads/paper/91c1d3dc3dcc3ead7da1e3972434a8d9.pdf)
  - **Before instruction**, pooled across concepts:
    - Complete understanding: 14.3%
    - Incomplete: 33.3%
    - Misconception: 18.5%
    - No understanding: 33.9%
  - So **52.4% held either a misconception or no understanding**.
  - After 18 periods of reasoning-based activities with models and data: complete 63.7%, incomplete 29.7%, misconception 4.2%, no understanding 2.4%.
  - Documented misconceptions:
    - **Moon phases** happen because "the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon".
    - One student said phases happen "because it is วันพระ / วันข้างขึ้น" (a Buddhist holy day or waxing day).
    - **Seasons** come from Earth's elliptical orbit and distance from the Sun.
    - **Tides**: high tide happens at night and low tide in the day "because the Moon rises at night and sets in the day". Others believed tides come only from the Moon orbiting or the Earth rotating.
  - The author attributes the tide misconceptions partly to students living far from the sea, with no direct experience.
  - The author explicitly attributes the problems to astronomy being abstract and requiring spatial imagination. His literature review says students at every level, *and teachers*, hold basic astronomy misconceptions.

**Other Thai evidence (cited secondhand in Jansri & Ketpichainarong 2020, [IJEM](https://www.ijem.com/articles/IJEM_6_4_745.pdf))**
- Dahsah et al. (2012): most Thai high-school students cannot explain the relationship in Earth–Moon–Sun models.
- Anantasook et al. (2015): Thai Grade 9 students could not relate classroom knowledge to explain the relation of the Sun, the Moon and the stars ("Thai Students' Understanding about Celestial Motion within Their Social and Cultural Context").
- Narjaikaew et al. (2016): Thai science teachers held many misconceptions about seasons in different areas of the Earth.

**Thai primary students (qualitative, small)**
- Grade 3 (ป.3) Thai students thought phases come from the observer's position changing as Earth rotates while the Moon stays still. — [inquiringmind.in.th](https://www.inquiringmind.in.th/archives/628)

### Inferences
- Thai and international data agree: the eclipse/Earth-shadow model is the default misconception. LUNARIS's 3D Moon lit by the *true* Sun position directly shows that the terminator (the line between the lit and dark halves of the Moon) comes from the Sun's direction, not from Earth's shadow. That makes it a targeted conceptual-change tool.
- In Thailand, Moon phases are culturally tied to the lunar calendar and Buddhist holy days (ข้างขึ้น/ข้างแรม, วันพระ). The Thai misconception data even includes a "วันพระ" explanation, so a culturally anchored hook exists.
- Thai improvement in the Sisaket study came from *model-based and data-based* reasoning activities. This supports the LUNARIS approach.

### Gaps
- **I found no large-sample (n > 100) Thai survey of secondary students' Moon-phase or eclipse misconceptions with percentages.** Thai studies found are small-sample (n = 26–28) or cited secondhand. Searches of ThaiJO via web search returned mostly mathematics misconception studies.
- Thai data on "why we always see the same side of the Moon" (synchronous rotation) and on "day length on the Moon": **none found**.
- Full texts of Dahsah et al. (2012), Anantasook et al. (2015) and Narjaikaew et al. (2016) were not retrieved, so I have no percentages from them.
- Recommended follow-up: search ThaiJO directly (tci-thaijo.org) for "ดวงจันทร์", "ข้างขึ้นข้างแรม", "two-tier" and "แบบวัดแนวคิด" in science-education journals (e.g., วารสารศึกษาศาสตร์ มข., วารสารครุศาสตร์ จุฬาฯ), plus Chula CUIR theses.

## Q5. International baseline research on Moon-phase misconceptions

### Takeaway
Internationally, 90%+ of untaught pre-service elementary teachers hold non-scientific ideas about Moon phases. The Earth's-shadow ("eclipse") model is the most common one across more than 3,000 participants in 11+ studies. Inquiry instruction with 3D models and observation substantially fixes this.

### Cited Findings
- **Trundle, Atwood & Christopher (2002)**, *J. Research in Science Teaching* 39(7):633–658; n = 78 US pre-service elementary teachers. — [full text PDF](https://indico.unina.it/event/73/contributions/1042/attachments/359/631/2002_-_Preservice_Elementary_Teachers_Conceptions_of_Moon_Phases.pdf)
  - Before instruction, **38 of 42 (90.5%)** in the physics groups and **14 of 15 (93.3%)** in the methods group held alternative conceptions or fragments of them.
  - "The most frequently encountered alternative conception within all groups... was that the earth's shadow causes the phases of the moon (eclipse)". The authors say this is consistent with **11 other studies involving over 3,000 participants at all levels of schooling**.
  - Before instruction, only 35 of 57 (61.4%) knew the Moon orbits the Earth.
  - After inquiry instruction (McDermott's *Physics by Inquiry*, with Moon observation), **43 of 63 (68.3%)** held a fully scientific conception, and 59 of 63 (93.7%) held scientific or near-scientific ones.
- Prior studies summarised in Trundle et al. (2002):
  - Callison & Wright (1993), pre-service teachers (n = 64): **6.6%** scientific before instruction, 22.4% after.
  - Schoon (1995): **18% of 122** pre-service elementary teachers understood Moon phases.
  - Sadler (1987): **37% of 213 Grade 9–12** students understood the cause before instruction.
  - Targan (1988), college non-science majors (n = 61): 1.6% before, 18% after. — [Trundle et al. 2002](https://indico.unina.it/event/73/contributions/1042/attachments/359/631/2002_-_Preservice_Elementary_Teachers_Conceptions_of_Moon_Phases.pdf)
- Fanetti (2001): **21% of 376** students said Moon phases are caused by Earth's shadow. — cited in [Jindawong 2012](http://backoffice.thaiedresearch.org/uploads/paper/91c1d3dc3dcc3ead7da1e3972434a8d9.pdf)
- Yalcin, Yalcin & Isleyen (2012), Turkey: pre-service primary science teachers' understanding of Moon phases and lunar eclipses is weak. Most attribute phases to Earth's shadow, and traditional instruction alone is insufficient. — [Procedia – Social and Behavioral Sciences 55](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1877042812040311)
- Turkey: 42% of pre-service science teachers said the Moon moves into Earth's shadow while phases occur (Kanlı 2014, cited secondarily). — [ERIC ED610974](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED610974.pdf) (from a search snippet only; not opened)
- **Lunar Phases Concept Inventory (LPCI)**, Lindell & Olsen (2002): a multiple-choice instrument for diagnosing students' mental models of lunar phases, field-tested at five US institutions. — [PhysPort LPCI page](https://www.physport.org/assessments/LPCI)

### Inferences
- International norms set the expectation. Without targeted model-based instruction, most learners (and most teachers) will default to the Earth-shadow model. That justifies a dedicated visual/3D intervention rather than more textbook text.
- The LPCI could be adapted or translated as the pre/post instrument for the LUNARIS pilot evaluation. Judges at an education faculty will expect a validated instrument.

### Gaps
- Bailey & Slater (2003) "A Review of Astronomy Education Research" (*Astronomy Education Review*) was **not retrieved** in this session. Cite it only after checking.
- LPCI field-test statistics from Lindell & Olsen (2002) were not retrieved.

## Q6. Teacher-side problems teaching astronomy in Thailand

### Takeaway
The best Thai evidence is Jansri & Ketpichainarong (2020): 45 Thai high-school science teachers, all teaching astronomy, none trained in astronomy. Large shares of them held misconceptions, and they named three obstacles: (1) weak content knowledge and low confidence, leading to lecture/textbook-reading; (2) an overloaded, abstract curriculum and textbooks; (3) a lack of materials, equipment and class time, plus urban cloudy skies that block observation. PISA 2025 adds that only 46.6% of Thai students attend schools reporting no teacher shortage.

### Cited Findings
- **Sample**: Jansri & Ketpichainarong (Mahidol University), *International Journal of Educational Methodology* 6(4):745–758 (2020), n = **45 in-service high-school science teachers** from across Thailand. "All teachers taught astronomy at the secondary school level, but their educational background was not related to astronomy." — [IJEM PDF](https://www.ijem.com/articles/IJEM_6_4_745.pdf)
  - Teachers' degree subjects were physics 6, biology 3, chemistry 4, general science 9, physical science 4, B.Ed. 5 and M.Ed. 14.
  - 23 of 45 had taught astronomy for only 1–5 years. 11 reported "never" (even though the paper says all taught astronomy).
- **Results before training:**
  - Cause of seasons: only **9%** had complete understanding; **42%** were "confused" and 33% incomplete. Most attributed seasons to Earth–Sun distance.
  - Sun's path at Bangkok (15°N) across solstices and equinoxes: only **7%** drew it correctly, and 69% had incomplete understanding. **40%** believed the Sun always rises due east and sets due west.
  - Celestial sphere reference points: 13% complete. Locating stars: 18% complete. — [IJEM](https://www.ijem.com/articles/IJEM_6_4_745.pdf)
- **Three obstacle themes**, quoted from interviews — [IJEM](https://www.ijem.com/articles/IJEM_6_4_745.pdf):
  1. **Content knowledge and confidence.** "Most of science teachers didn't graduate from astronomical field" (T5). "I had no confidence in teaching astronomy... I just stood in front of the classroom and explained what are described in the textbook or wrote what the students supposed to know on the blackboard" (T7). This is direct evidence of lecture/textbook reliance.
  2. **Curriculum and textbook difficulty.** The curriculum has too many topics, and the textbooks are "not interesting and containing too many details" (T4). Students study astronomy mainly because it is on O-NET (T3).
  3. **Materials, facilities and time.** "My school lacks science equipment, so I just chose only simple activities" (T9). There is too little class time for the activities (T2, T7).
- **Observation barrier (urban sky/clouds):** "especially those who live in the city, where the sky is closed and cloudy, resulted in the failure of the observation of the Sun's and stars' positions" (T8). — [IJEM](https://www.ijem.com/articles/IJEM_6_4_745.pdf)
- **Curriculum mismatch:** the Thai curriculum teaches four seasons, but Thailand has three (hot, cool, rainy). The authors say "textbooks may promote misconceptions both in students and teachers". — [IJEM](https://www.ijem.com/articles/IJEM_6_4_745.pdf)
- Thai pre-service teachers (KKU, n = 26): only 7.7% had sound understanding of Moon phases (see Q4). The authors note that "if teacher's conceptions are diverging from scientific concept, it may be very difficult to teach a correct concept to the students". — [Saenpuk & Ruangsuwan](https://indico.global/event/8304/papers/76194/files/1891-98.The_pre-service_teachers_understanding_about_moon_phase.pdf)
- PISA 2025 (Thailand): only **46.6%** of students are in schools reporting no teacher shortage, and only **36.8%** in schools reporting no shortage of learning materials. — [The Nation](https://www.nationthailand.com/news/policy/40070867)
- IPST's official response to PISA 2022 prioritised "เร่งพัฒนาสมรรถนะครูจัดการเรียนรู้" (urgently developing teachers' instructional competencies). — [IPST](https://www.ipst.ac.th/news/54912/20231106-pisa2022.html)
- NARIT runs astronomy teacher-training workshops for science teachers, which suggests a recognised training need. — [NARIT K-12 Astronomy Education Conference in Asia, Sept 2025](https://indico.narit.or.th/event/221/timetable/?view=standard) (exists per search results; contents not reviewed)

### Inferences
- **Daytime school hours and observing the Moon:** I found no Thai study that measures this directly. However, the IJEM teachers' complaints (urban cloudy skies, no equipment, limited class time) and the general fact that Moon-phase observation needs observations across a month, often at night, together support the claim that real-sky observation is impractical in Thai classrooms. A data-driven simulator using the real Sun position fills that gap. Frame this as a reasoned inference, not a measured finding.
- Thai teachers' content gaps (seasons, Sun's path) sit in the same Sun–Earth geometry that LUNARIS's Sun/Earth sky charts visualise. The tool could double as teacher professional development.

### Gaps
- **National out-of-field statistics for Earth/space science specifically**: not found. A search snippet cited out-of-field rates of 26% (mathematics), 26% (social studies), 24% (Thai), 20% (English) and 15% (science), but I could not identify or verify the source. **Do not cite it without tracing it.**
- No Thai survey of teacher self-efficacy specific to astronomy with larger samples was found. Jansri & Ketpichainarong is qualitative on confidence.
- No direct data on light pollution or the daytime-schedule barrier in Thai schools.

## Q7. Educational inequality in Thailand (supports a no-equipment, web-based tool)

### Takeaway
Thai science outcomes are highly unequal. In PISA 2022, only university demonstration schools exceeded the OECD average, and top and bottom students differ by about 200 points. Roughly one million children attend about 15,000 small, mostly rural schools. Rural students lag urban students by more than a year of schooling in science (PISA 2015); one report claims about three years in PISA 2022 (unverified).

### Cited Findings
- PISA 2022, Thailand ([IPST](https://www.ipst.ac.th/news/54912/20231106-pisa2022.html)):
  - Only **university demonstration schools** scored above the OECD average in mathematics and science. All other school groups were below it in all three subjects.
  - The 90th and 10th percentiles differ by **about 200 points** in every subject.
  - 15% of students are "resilient" (bottom SES quartile but top quartile in mathematics), against 10% in the OECD.
- Small schools (World Bank blog, Sondergaard & Lathapipat, 22 Jan 2017 — [World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/eastasiapacific/providing-better-education-children-thailand-s-small-schools)):
  - **About 15,000 Thai schools have fewer than 120 students**, out of ~30,000 schools.
  - **Nearly one million children** attend small, mostly rural schools.
  - OBEC identified 10,971 small schools as consolidation candidates.
  - PISA 2015: "science learning in rural areas is behind urban areas by **more than a year of schooling**".
- PISA 2022: rural 15-year-olds lag urban peers by **about three school years** in mathematics, science and reading. — [Thairath English](https://en.thairath.co.th/scoop/theissue/2908035) (**unverified**: from a search summary only; not opened; check against OECD data before citing)

### Inferences
- A browser-based tool that needs no telescope, planetarium or clear night sky lowers the resource barrier that small and rural schools face. A materials shortage is reported for about 63% of Thai students (100% − 36.8%).
- Elite demonstration schools are the only group above the OECD level, so the "equity" argument is strong: a high-quality, free simulation can bring demonstration-school-style model-based learning to ordinary OBEC schools.
