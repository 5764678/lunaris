# Practical constraints in Thai secondary schools for a web-based 3D science platform (LUNARIS)

Research date: 11 Nov 2026. Scope: Thailand, preferring 2022–2026 data. Where only older data exists (e.g., OBEC 2020 connectivity, PISA 2018 school ICT), it is flagged. Items marked **[UNVERIFIED]** came from search-result summaries that I could not open and check against the primary document.

---

## 1. Device and connectivity access in Thai schools and homes

### Takeaway
Almost every OBEC school has some internet connection, but nobody publishes data on school bandwidth or its quality. About one-third of principals, and more in rural, public and disadvantaged schools, report that bandwidth or devices are insufficient. Schools average about 17 students per computer. Students reach the internet mainly through smartphones, and about 4 in 10 15-year-olds had no computer at home (PISA 2018). The flagship 2025–2031 "Anywhere Anytime" programme is now delivering leased devices (iPads, Android tablets, notebooks and Chromebooks) to upper-secondary (M.4–6) students. Rollout is slow and uneven, and lower-secondary, small and remote schools come last.

### Cited Findings

**School connectivity (latest systematic data is OBEC 2020, published by ITU in 2022)**
- Of OBEC schools (78.65% of institutions, 52% of learners), 97.47% (28,889 schools) were connected to the internet. 0.8% (242 schools) were not connected, and data was missing for 1.7% (509). The unconnected schools were small, averaging 30 students. — [ITU, *E-learning in Thailand: Mapping the digital divide* (2022), prepared with OBEC/NSO/MDES/NBTC/UNICEF/UNESCO](https://thailand.un.org/sites/default/files/2022-09/21-00630_E-learning-Thailand-Mapping-digital-divide%5B74%5D.pdf)
- About 70% of OBEC schools connected by fibre, and nearly 6% (1,672) relied on 3G/4G cellular, with a slightly higher share in the North. OBEC's dataset holds **no information on school internet speed or quality.** — [ITU 2022](https://thailand.un.org/sites/default/files/2022-09/21-00630_E-learning-Thailand-Mapping-digital-divide%5B74%5D.pdf)
- PISA 2018 principal data, as analysed by ITU: one in three principals felt bandwidth was insufficient. Sufficiency was 70% in public schools vs 93% in private, 66% rural vs 85% urban, and 63% in disadvantaged vs 86% in advantaged schools. 87% of students reported school Wi-Fi access (95% top vs 77% bottom socio-economic quartile). — [ITU 2022](https://thailand.un.org/sites/default/files/2022-09/21-00630_E-learning-Thailand-Mapping-digital-divide%5B74%5D.pdf)
- ITU's per-device bandwidth benchmarks are 0.25–0.5 Mbit/s for web browsing, 0.5–1.4 Mbit/s for online web-based learning and 1.5–5.0 Mbit/s for HD video. It also cites a US benchmark (SETDA) of 1.4–2.8 Mbit/s per user. — [ITU 2022](https://thailand.un.org/sites/default/files/2022-09/21-00630_E-learning-Thailand-Mapping-digital-divide%5B74%5D.pdf)
- In a 2016 depa survey of 923 OBEC/BMA schools (science-teacher respondents), 99% of schools had "high-speed" internet, but 65% of teachers said it was too slow. 55% of schools had no computer specialist. About half of students could access only a smartphone. *Old data (2016), useful for showing the pattern only.* — [depa survey reported by Matichon via hongpakkroo.com (Oct 2017)](https://www.hongpakkroo.com/2965.html)
- A 2025 policy analysis reports that school signals are often weak or unstable when many users connect at once. Some schools pay for extra connectivity from their own budgets, and some teachers pay for their own mobile data. — [Thorn Pitidon, kidforkids.org / EEF-supported (18 Sep 2025)](https://kidforkids.org/anywhere-anytime/)
- NBTC's USO programme had given Wi-Fi to 4,380 schools and set up 1,623 internet centres at schools, a total of over 6,000 schools (2020 data). Net Pracharat village fibre also reaches schools, but the project "does not identify the schools clearly." — [UN/ITU presentation, Mapping the digital divide (2021)](https://thailand.un.org/sites/default/files/2021-08/Presentation%20on%20mapping%20of%20digital%20divide.pdf); [Telecom Review Asia](https://www.telecomreviewasia.com/news/featured-articles/27822-thailands-push-for-universal-internet-access/)
- The OBEC 2019 news plan aimed to install 1 Gbps "high-speed" internet in all schools by AY2020. It was a plan, not a measured outcome. — [Thai Post (2019)](https://www.thaipost.net/main/detail/49984)

**National network speeds (context; these are urban-weighted medians, not school-level figures)**
- Thailand's fixed broadband median download was 273.78 Mbps (Feb 2026, Ookla, 3rd in ASEAN). — [SEAsia.co, Apr 2026](https://seasia.co/2026/04/20/asean-internet-speeds-index-february-2026-big-condolences-to-indonesian-users)
- Mobile median download was 136.91 Mbps (Nov 2025, Ookla). — [Bisnis.com, Dec 2025](https://teknologi.bisnis.com/read/20251222/101/1938772/kecepatan-internet-seluler-ri-november-2025-lebih-cepat-dari-kamboja-dan-laos)

**Computers in schools**
- On average 17 students shared one school computer (OBEC data, c. 2020). The figure was 19 in the South and 16 in the Central region, ranging from 27 in Narathiwat and Yala to 12 in Ang Thong and Sing Buri. — [ITU 2022](https://thailand.un.org/sites/default/files/2022-09/21-00630_E-learning-Thailand-Mapping-digital-divide%5B74%5D.pdf)
- PISA 2018: 40% of Thai students said no notebook was available for them at school. Only 56% of public-school principals said their school had enough digital devices for instruction, against 83% in private schools. 64% of rural principals said devices had sufficient computing capacity, against 72% of urban principals. — [ITU 2022](https://thailand.un.org/sites/default/files/2022-09/21-00630_E-learning-Thailand-Mapping-digital-divide%5B74%5D.pdf)
- OECD Education GPS (PISA 2022) ranks Thailand 34th of 40 on change in computers per student between 2012 and 2022, a small increase. It ranks Thailand 4th of 70 on principal-reported "preparedness for digital learning". — [OECD Education GPS, Thailand](https://gpseducation.oecd.org/CountryProfile?primaryCountry=THA&treshold=10&topic=PI)
- EEF's review of PISA 2022 says Thailand's computers-per-student ratio is below the OECD average. — [EEF Research, Lessons from PISA 2022](https://research.eef.or.th/lessons-from-pisa-2022-what-characterizes-a-resilient-education-system/)
- **[UNVERIFIED]** PISA 2022 Thailand: 71% of students use digital tools for learning ≥1 hour a day at school (OECD 55%). 55% spend more than 1 hour a day on digital *leisure* at school (OECD 35%). The Philippines (71%) and Thailand (68%) have the highest shares of "excessive users". These figures came from a search summary of OECD PISA 2022 materials, and I did not locate the exact table. — [OECD, *Finite time to learn and play* (2025)](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2025/10/finite-time-to-learn-and-play_8dd83eff/edbaa4bb-en.pdf)
- PISA 2022 country note (verified): 26% of Thai students get distracted using digital devices in maths lessons (OECD 30%). Only 51% felt confident using video-communication programs for remote learning (OECD 77%). 41% had school buildings closed for more than 3 months during COVID. — [OECD PISA 2022 Factsheet, Thailand](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2023/11/pisa-2022-results-volume-i-and-ii-country-notes_2fca04b9/thailand_b136aea5/6138f4af-en.pdf)
- PISA 2022 science (verified): only 47% of Thai students reached Level 2 or above in science (OECD 76%), and 1% were top performers (OECD 7%). 33% of students were in the bottom international socio-economic quintile. — [OECD PISA 2022 Factsheet, Thailand](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2023/11/pisa-2022-results-volume-i-and-ii-country-notes_2fca04b9/thailand_b136aea5/6138f4af-en.pdf)

**Home access and devices students actually use**
- NSO Q4/2024: of about 66.1 million people aged 6+, 60.0 million (90.9%) used the internet and 63.2 million (95.7%) used a mobile phone. 92.6% of 24.3 million households were connected. — [Thailandplus summary of NSO Q4/2567](https://www.thailandplus.tv/archives/901671); [NSO report PDF](https://www.nso.go.th/nsoweb/storage/survey_detail/2025/20250228090924_27941.pdf) **[UNVERIFIED in detail; the NSO PDF exceeded the fetch size limit, so the figures come from search summaries]**
- NSO Q1/2024: 99.1% of people aged 15–24 used the internet and 99.2% used a mobile phone. — [NSO PDF (2024)](https://www.nso.go.th/nsoweb/storage/survey_detail/2024/20240531072624_48392.pdf) **[UNVERIFIED, from search summary]**
- PISA 2018: about 4 in 10 Thai 15-year-olds had **neither** a laptop nor a desktop at home, and 85% had home internet (11 points below the OECD average). — [ITU 2022](https://thailand.un.org/sites/default/files/2022-09/21-00630_E-learning-Thailand-Mapping-digital-divide%5B74%5D.pdf)

- A student at Suankularb Wittayalai Nonthaburi said he must bring a phone to school daily because teachers run lessons through Google Classroom. — [Thai PBS World, 11 Nov 2024](https://www.thaipbsworld.com/education/free-tablet-device-scheme...-a-solution-or-another-problem%3F)
- Dec 2024: EEF (กสศ.) distributed 357,000 "ซิมพร้อมเรียน" (ready-to-learn) data SIMs to poor students nationwide. — [The Bangkok Insight](https://www.thebangkokinsight.com/news/digital-economy/1425071/)

**Government device programmes ("Anywhere Anytime" / เรียนดี มีความสุข)**
- On 28 Jan 2025 the Cabinet approved 29,765,253,600 THB for FY2026–2031 (phase 2) for tablets, laptops and other devices on a **rental** basis, plus high-quality internet. The 2025 target was more than 600,000 M.4–6 students in community-quality and opportunity-expansion schools. The plan extends to the remaining ~1.2 million upper-secondary students and to lower-secondary students in quality schools. — [The Nation, 29 Jan 2025](https://www.nationthailand.com/news/general/40045699)
- The US Commerce Dept describes a 6-year (2026–2031), 3-phase initiative of about US$800 million for 2.6 million tablets and laptops, with phase 1 at about US$250 million covering 600k+ students. — [trade.gov Market Intelligence, 22 Aug 2025](https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/thailand-education-and-procurement-electronic-devices)
- Original budget lines (million THB): student devices 44,420; teacher devices 1,683; new content 1,347; **new learning platform + cloud 4,505**; training only **8**. The tender was suspended in late June 2025. Procurement then moved to the education service areas (ESAs), and the central **platform procurement was halted**. In 2025 only 1,018 schools were covered, mostly already well-equipped ones. Small and remote schools were left for later rounds. The TOR included an unlimited education-content SIM plus 30 GB a month of general data per student. — [kidforkids.org (EEF/101 PUB/ThaiHealth), 18 Sep 2025](https://kidforkids.org/anywhere-anytime/)
- 21 Oct 2025: the OBEC Secretary-General (Phichet Phophakdi) said devices (tablets, notebooks, Chromebooks) for about 600,000 M.4–6 students and their teachers would be leased through 118 ESAs. The project was "still in planning" and targeted the second semester. — [Daily News, 21 Oct 2025](https://www.dailynews.co.th/news/5224071/)
- Delivery evidence: Sangkha School (Surin) handed **iPads** to 1,198 M.4–6 students on 18–19 Feb 2026 under Anywhere Anytime, and Weerawat Yothin School handed out iPads on 16–20 Feb 2026. — [Sangkha School](https://sangkha.ac.th/4306/); [Weerawat Yothin School](https://weerawat.ac.th/5708/)
- Precedent: the 2012 One Tablet per Child programme distributed more than 850,000 devices (about 2 bn THB), suffered delays and bid-rigging allegations, and was terminated in 2014. — [Thai PBS World, 11 Nov 2024](https://www.thaipbsworld.com/education/free-tablet-device-scheme...-a-solution-or-another-problem%3F)

**Rural/small-school gap**
- AY2021: 14,950 of about 29,500 OBEC schools (50.68%) were "small schools" with 120 students or fewer, and the share is rising (citing OBEC 2022). — [Chophayom Journal, Rajabhat Mahasarakham (ThaiJO)](https://so01.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/ejChophayom/article/download/285776/182535/1204749)
- **[UNVERIFIED]** AY2024: 15,327 of 29,152 OBEC schools (52.58%) were small schools, according to a search summary of a ThaiJO article that I could not locate.

### Inferences
- Upper-secondary classes in 2026–27 will hold a **mixed fleet**: leased iPads (Safari/WebKit), Android tablets, Chromebooks, Windows notebooks, students' own (often budget) Android phones, and shared lab PCs. Lower-secondary (M.1–3) students, the main audience for the core astronomy strand, mostly **do not** have government devices yet and will use phones or a shared lab or projector.
- School internet exists almost everywhere, but per-device bandwidth when 30–40 students load at once is the binding constraint. With ~0.5–1.4 Mbit/s per device as a benchmark and many schools on shared or 4G links, the app should be playable after a **small initial download (a few MB)**. Heavy assets should load progressively and be cached for reuse.
- The **teacher-projector mode** (one device, whole class) is realistic for small and opportunity-expansion schools, which hold half of OBEC schools.
- Back-end note (inference from the brief, not a Thai source): a Render free-plan back end that sleeps and takes about 1 minute to wake is incompatible with a 50-minute class period, where all students hit "start" at once. Core lesson content should be static or CDN-served and cacheable, with the API optional.

### Gaps
- No public 2023–2026 OBEC dataset on school bandwidth (Mbps) or per-school computer counts. ITU (2022) explicitly found that OBEC does not record speed. OBEC EMIS or the OBEC ICT bureau (สทร.) would need a direct request.
- I could not retrieve PISA 2022 Thailand ICT tables (e.g., Table II.B1.5.24, computers per student) directly. The figures above come from OECD Education GPS or are PISA 2018.
- NSO 2024/2025 child-specific (ages 6–14) computer and smartphone figures were not retrievable because the PDFs were too large or garbled.
- The status of Anywhere Anytime in Nov 2026 (devices delivered in total, whether lower-secondary started, platform status) is not confirmed beyond Feb 2026 school posts. The device technical specs (RAM, OS) from the ESA-level TORs were not found.
- NBTC USO / Net Pracharat school-connection counts for 2023–2026 were not found.

## 2. Typical school hardware and WebGL/3D performance implications

### Takeaway
WebGL is available on about 98–99% of browsers, including Android, so availability is not the problem. The real constraints are budget GPUs (e.g., Mali-G52-class phones), integrated graphics in lab PCs, thermal throttling on tablets, and shared bandwidth. A 3D app for Thai schools needs a strict performance budget, adaptive quality, and a 2D/static fallback.

### Cited Findings
- WebGL is supported on about 98.25% of browsers overall and about 99.03% on Android. WebGL2 is supported on about 97.53% overall and about 99.23% on Android. About 3.5% of devices run with "reduced performance" (major performance caveats). — [web3dsurvey.com WebGL](https://web3dsurvey.com/webgl); [web3dsurvey.com WebGL2](https://web3dsurvey.com/webgl2)
- WebGL 2 is missing only on very old devices (iOS before 15, Android before 8). A 2020 budget Android phone with a Mali-G52 GPU will struggle with complex shaders and large textures. — [abratabia.com, Mobile WebGL support](https://www.abratabia.com/mobile-web-games/mobile-webgl-support.php)
- Thai government computer procurement follows the MDES/MOE "เกณฑ์ราคากลางและคุณลักษณะพื้นฐานการจัดหาอุปกรณ์และระบบคอมพิวเตอร์" (price ceilings and *minimum* specs). For example, the 2023 edition specifies an all-in-one "for processing work" at 24,000 THB with a ≥6-core CPU. A December 2025 edition exists. — [yotathai.com (criteria Dec 2568)](https://www.yotathai.com/yotanews/rakaklang-computer68); [Mahidol TM copy of TH-2566 criteria](https://www.tm.mahidol.ac.th/it-new/sites/default/files/TH-2566.pdf)
- Before the 2025 lease programme, the stock of school computers was old and thin. 40% of students had no notebook available at school (PISA 2018), and 1 in 3 public-school principals lacked enough devices (see Section 1). — [ITU 2022](https://thailand.un.org/sites/default/files/2022-09/21-00630_E-learning-Thailand-Mapping-digital-divide%5B74%5D.pdf)

### Inferences
- Procurement price ceilings and "minimum spec" criteria mean school lab PCs and all-in-ones almost always use **integrated graphics** (Intel UHD/Iris or AMD APU). This is an inference from the spec framework; no Thai survey of school GPUs was found.
- Suggested budgets for LUNARIS, as engineering targets rather than sourced figures:
  - Initial payload of 3 MB or less (gzipped JS plus a low-res Moon) before first interaction.
  - LOD textures of 1K on phones and 2K/4K only on request.
  - Fewer than 100 draw calls and capped device pixel ratio (≤1.5) on mobile.
  - A target of 30 fps on a Mali-G52-class phone or 4 GB-RAM tablet.
  - Automatic quality downgrade when frame time exceeds about 40 ms.
  - A static 2D (image or SVG) mode when WebGL context creation fails or the device reports a low-end GPU.
- iPads delivered under Anywhere Anytime run Safari (WebKit), so the 3D layer must be tested on iPadOS Safari, not only Chrome on Android.
- PWA or offline caching (service worker plus pre-cached lesson packs) supports patchy rural links and reduces the load from 30+ simultaneous downloads.

### Gaps
- No Thai dataset on the RAM/GPU distribution of students' phones or the specs of Anywhere Anytime tablets and Chromebooks (the TORs are set per ESA and were not found).
- No published three.js-specific mobile performance budget from an authoritative source. The budgets above are engineering judgement.

## 3. Teacher digital competence, readiness, workload and adoption drivers

### Takeaway
Thai teachers already use technology mainly for **lesson planning**. They lack a guiding framework for systematic integration, and they are badly overloaded with non-teaching work, especially in small schools. Teachers adopt tools that cut preparation time: Thai-language, curriculum-aligned, ready-to-teach material. National digital-competency efforts (MOE/UNICEF framework, IPST online training) are still being built.

### Cited Findings
- In a July 2025 MOE–UNICEF Thailand–EdTech Hub workshop on a national digital competency framework for education, a ONE TEACHER Thailand survey (n=256) found teachers use technology mainly for lesson planning (80%), in-class instruction (68%) and assessment through grading or LMS (38%). **74% said they lacked a guiding framework for systematically integrating digital tools.** The framework has 9 competency domains, Chulalongkorn University supplied facilitators, and OBEC, IPST, OVEC and others took part. — [EdTech Hub, 7 Aug 2025](https://edtechhub.org/2025/08/07/building-competencies-for-a-digital-age)
- PISA 2018 principal data: most principals agreed teachers had the skills to integrate digital devices, and about three-quarters said teachers had enough time to prepare digital lessons. In the bottom socio-economic quartile, however, one in three principals said teachers **lacked time** (vs 12% in the top quartile). Nearly one in three disadvantaged-school principals said no professional resources existed for teachers to learn digital devices (vs 8% in advantaged schools). — [ITU 2022](https://thailand.un.org/sites/default/files/2022-09/21-00630_E-learning-Thailand-Mapping-digital-divide%5B74%5D.pdf)
- Workload: a TDRI-led study found Thai teachers are away from class up to 84 days a year on non-teaching activities, with paperwork for assessments taking about 40% of their time (43 days a year). *Dated (2015); Wikipedia attributes the 84-day figure to the Quality Learning Foundation.* — [TDRI, 2015](https://tdri.or.th/en/2015/05/education-reform-first-put-teachers-back-in-the-classroom/)
- EEFI/EEF study (2026): small-school teachers teach an average of **27.31 hours a week, 37.6% above the MOE standard**, often across several subjects and grades at once, plus administrative and other duties. — [EEF, "The Small-School Teacher Crisis", 13 Jul 2026](https://en.eef.or.th/2026/07/13/the-small-school-teacher-crisis/) (page returned 403 to the fetcher, so the figures were confirmed via [Thansettakij](https://www.thansettakij.com/general-news/649108))
- The OECD's 2016 review found the paperwork tied to school assessments keeps Thai teachers from focusing on learning. — [OECD, Education in Thailand (2016)](https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2016/08/education-in-thailand_g1g6a08c/9789264259119-en.pdf)
- In the depa 2016 survey, teachers mostly used YouTube, Microsoft Office and OBEC and IPST websites. 31% had never created digital media, and 57% of self-made media was shared only inside their school. *Old data.* — [hongpakkroo.com citing Matichon/depa](https://www.hongpakkroo.com/2965.html)
- The Anywhere Anytime budget assigned only 8 million THB to training against about 46 bn THB for devices. Critics flag the risk that devices get used for games without teacher guidance. — [kidforkids.org, Sep 2025](https://kidforkids.org/anywhere-anytime/)
- IPST runs an online teacher-training system (ระบบอบรมครู) for science, maths and technology PD, with a 2025 service manual and a guide for course creators. This is a channel through which a LUNARIS teacher course could be offered. — [IPST service manual (2025)](https://www.ipst.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/คู่มือการให้บริการระบบอบรมครู.pdf); [IPST course-creator manual 2568](https://www.ipst.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/คู่มือการใช้งานระบบอบรมครูสำหรับผู้สร้างหลักสูตร-ปฏิบัติ-2568.pdf)
- Regional ThaiJO studies (2023) of teacher digital competence find self-rated competence at "high" levels with gaps by age. One example is PE teachers rating themselves at a mean of about 4.15/5. These are small, local samples. — [SNRU journal, 2023](https://jci.snru.ac.th/Files/Article/1084-ArticleTextFile-20230927053925.pdf); [JLIT needs assessment, 345 Bangkok-area secondary teachers](https://so06.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/JLIT/article/view/281301)
- A study of 400 BMA primary EFL teachers found digital-technology integration at a "moderate" level. — [ThaiJO JONW](https://so06.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/jonw/article/view/293838)

### Inferences
- The strongest adoption lever is a **complete teaching kit** in Thai: แผนการจัดการเรียนรู้ (lesson plans in the OBEC format), ใบงาน (worksheets), ใบความรู้ (knowledge sheets), assessment rubrics, slides and teacher notes. It should be explicitly mapped to the IPST science curriculum indicators (ตัวชี้วัด, the earth and space strand). Teachers then add no planning load, and the kit can serve as evidence for PA (วิทยฐานะ) teacher-evaluation portfolios. The PA link is inference and was not verified in this research.
- Lessons should fit one 50-minute period, with no teacher account setup and no student accounts.
- Provide a 1–2-hour teacher PD micro-course (via IPST's system or a short video series). This addresses the 74% who lack an integration framework and the tiny training budget.

### Gaps
- No 2023–2026 nationally representative survey of Thai teacher digital competence (percentages by level) was found. OBEC and the Office of the Education Council would need direct requests. Thailand's participation in TALIS 2024 was not checked.
- No hard data on how many science teachers in small or opportunity-expansion schools teach out of field. This matters for how much scaffolding astronomy content needs.
- No IPST statistics on the number of teachers trained online.

## 4. Language: Thai localisation, terminology, bilingual value

### Takeaway
English proficiency in Thailand is among the lowest in Asia, so an English-only interface is a hard barrier for most students and many teachers. The platform should be Thai-first, use Royal Society (ราชบัณฑิตยสภา) standard astronomy terms, and offer an optional English toggle for EP/MEP and bilingual programmes.

### Cited Findings
- EF EPI 2025 ranks Thailand 116th of 123 with a score of 402, in the "very low" band, down 13 points from 2024. Reading was strongest (416) and writing weakest (363). The EF 2026 fact sheet lists Thailand at #107 with a score of 406. The index is based on self-selected test takers. — [Thaiger, 2025](https://thethaiger.com/news/national/thailand-english-proficiency-2025); [EF EPI 2025 Thailand fact sheet](https://www.ef.com/assetscdn/WIBIwq6RdJvcD9bc8RMd/cefcom-epi-site/fact-sheets/2025/ef-epi-fact-sheet-thailand-english.pdf); [EF EPI 2026 Thailand fact sheet](https://www.ef.com/assetscdn/WIBIwq6RdJvcD9bc8RMd/cefcom-epi-site/fact-sheets/2026/ef-epi-fact-sheet-thailand-english.pdf)
- The Office of the Royal Society publishes *พจนานุกรมศัพท์ดาราศาสตร์ ฉบับราชบัณฑิตยสถาน* (Dictionary of Astronomy Terms), 592 pages, 2024 printing. — [Chulabook listing](https://www.chulabook.com/en/education/205472)
- The Royal Society also runs an online coined-terms (ศัพท์บัญญัติ) search service. Whether it includes an astronomy category was not confirmed. — [Office of the Royal Society](https://www.orst.go.th/royin/?m=i); [Royal Institute vocabulary search description](https://www.amphur.in.th/the-royal-institute-vocabulary-search-tool)
- National content platforms (OBEC Content Center, DLTV, IPST) are Thai-language, and teachers rely on them heavily (Section 6).

### Inferences
- **Thai-first UI**: all interface text, labels, data tooltips, captions and narration in Thai. Thai fonts must be bundled or available (e.g., Sarabun or Noto Sans Thai, with correct line-breaking, since Thai has no spaces and needs ICU/`Intl.Segmenter` word breaking), and text-in-3D must render Thai glyphs, which matters for three.js text meshes and sprites.
- Feature names (craters, maria, Apollo sites) should follow the ราชบัณฑิตยสภา glossary, or NARIT usage where the Royal Society has no term, with English and IAU names shown in parentheses. This builds credibility with education-faculty judges.
- A bilingual toggle (TH/EN, with optional side-by-side glossary) is valuable for EP/MEP/IEP programmes and for building scientific English vocabulary. It also keeps LUNARIS's existing English content as an asset.

### Gaps
- Numbers of EP/MEP programmes and students in Thailand were not retrieved.
- NARIT's Thai astronomy glossaries and their relation to the Royal Society terms were not checked.
- No Thai study was found that quantifies the learning impact of Thai vs English interfaces in science software.

## 5. Legal and ethical constraints: PDPA, children's data, accessibility

### Takeaway
Under the PDPA (B.E. 2562), anyone under 20 is a minor. Consent-based processing requires parental consent for under-10s, and for 10–19-year-olds it requires the minor's consent plus parental consent unless the act is one a minor can do alone. Collecting student identities and learning analytics therefore triggers consent, notice and security duties for schools and the platform. The lowest-risk design is **no student accounts and no personal data**, with any analytics anonymous or aggregate. Thai government web standards reference WCAG 2.1/2.2.

### Cited Findings
- PDPA Section 20 (English translation): for a minor who is not sui juris by marriage or without capacity under Civil and Commercial Code s.27, consent must **also** come from the holder of parental responsibility where the act is not one the minor can do alone (CCC ss.22–24). **Where the minor is under 10, consent is given by the holder of parental responsibility.** — [pdpathailand.com, Section 20 (English)](https://pdpathailand.com/pdpa/content_eng/article20_eng.php)
- The age of majority is 20 (17 if lawfully married). Minors aged 10 or over need consent from both the minor and the parent, and under-10s need consent from the parent only. — [Nishimura & Asahi newsletter (2020)](https://www.nishimura.com/sites/default/files/images/en_newsletter_200630_2_corporate-crisis-management_asia.pdf); [Baker McKenzie, Legal bases (Thailand)](https://resourcehub.bakermckenzie.com/en/resources/global-data-and-cyber-handbook/asia-pacific/thailand/topics/legal-bases-for-processing-of-personal-data); [Linklaters, Data Protected – Thailand](https://www.linklaters.com/en/insights/data-protected/data-protected---thailand)
- The PDPC issued guidelines on consent and notification. Per Tilleke & Gibbins' summary, parental consent is not required in every case for the older (10–20) group. — [Tilleke & Gibbins](https://www.tilleke.com/insights/thailand-issues-guidelines-on-pdpa-consent-and-notification-requirements/) (page returned 503 when fetched, so this is a search summary)
- Consent is only one lawful basis. The PDPA also allows contract, legal obligation, public task and legitimate interest, which may apply to a school's own processing. Whether a school-directed basis applies to a third-party edtech tool needs legal review. — [Baker McKenzie](https://resourcehub.bakermckenzie.com/en/resources/global-data-and-cyber-handbook/asia-pacific/thailand/topics/legal-bases-for-processing-of-personal-data)
- Google Workspace for Education's terms require schools to obtain parental consent for under-18 students to use "additional services". This shows the global edtech norm that Thai schools already meet. — [Google Workspace Admin Help (Thai)](https://knowledge.workspace.google.com/admin/getting-started/editions/communicating-with-parents-and-guardians-about-google-workspace-for-education?hl=th)
- Accessibility: Thailand's DGA government-website standard (PH GovWebsite3) references WCAG 2.1 alongside TLS 1.3, and other Thai institutional guidelines (2025) reference WCAG 2.2. An NSTDA-led roadmap moves from awareness to a national standard to eventual legislation. — [DGA PH GovWebsite3 (2023)](https://standard.dga.or.th/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/PH-GovWebsite3_v.01.pdf); [HCU Accessibility Guideline 2025 v3.1](https://digital.hcu.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/Accessibility-Guideline-2025-V.3.1-Final.pdf); [NSTDA seminar (2024)](https://www.nstda.or.th/nac/2024/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/เสวนาการขับเคลื่อนให้เว็บไซต์ไทยเข้าถึงโดยสะดวกถ้วนหน้า-โดย-คุณรัตนา-จรูญศักดิ์สิทธิ์.pdf)
- Screen-time and well-being concerns: a child psychiatrist in Thai PBS coverage recommends offline activities and at least 1–2 hours of daily face-to-face interaction in device-pilot schools. — [Thai PBS World, Nov 2024](https://www.thaipbsworld.com/education/free-tablet-device-scheme...-a-solution-or-another-problem%3F)

### Inferences
- **Default mode is anonymous**: no login, no names, no student IDs. A teacher creates a class code, and students join with a nickname or seat number that is not stored server-side. Results show on the teacher's screen or are exported by the teacher, keeping the school as controller of any records.
- If accounts or analytics are added later, they need a PDPA privacy notice in Thai, a parental-consent flow (all secondary students are minors under 20), data minimisation, a Thai or regional hosting statement, a retention limit, and a processor agreement with the school. Leveraging the school's existing Google/Microsoft accounts via SSO, if used, would inherit their consent arrangements.
- Avoid third-party ad or tracking cookies, and use privacy-preserving, cookieless aggregate analytics. Judges in a Faculty of Education will expect explicit research ethics (IRB/consent) for any classroom trial data.
- Target WCAG 2.1 AA: colour-contrast on dark space scenes, keyboard and switch navigation for 3D controls, text alternatives or audio descriptions of 3D views, captions on video, and an adjustable font size. The 2D fallback doubles as an accessibility mode.

### Gaps
- The full text of the PDPC consent guideline and its date were not verified (503 error).
- No Thai-specific guidance was found on edtech processor obligations, or on whether OBEC has issued PDPA guidance for schools using third-party apps.
- No Thai law currently mandating WCAG for non-government educational sites was found.

## 6. National platforms and channels to plug into or distribute through

### Takeaway
Thai teachers already work inside a small set of channels: the MOE's DEEP platform (built with Google and Microsoft), OBEC Content Center, DLTV, IPST resources, Google Classroom, and above all LINE. A new tool gains reach by being linkable or embeddable into these, not by asking teachers to adopt another standalone portal. The 4.5 bn THB central "Anywhere Anytime" platform procurement was halted, leaving room for lightweight, interoperable content.

### Cited Findings
- **DEEP (Digital Education Excellence Platform)**: launched by the MOE jointly with Google and Microsoft (exact launch date not verified; sources place it around 2020–2021). It is an online learning hub for MOE teachers and personnel, students and the public, and includes teacher training. In 2023 the MOE invited teachers and students to register. — [The Standard](https://thestandard.co/moe-launched-deep-platform/); [Thai Post](https://www.thaipost.net/main/detail/65607); [MOE DEEP site](https://sites.google.com/moe.go.th/moe-deep/deep); [Samut Prakan ESA 2 notice](http://samut2.spn2.go.th/?p=17840)
- **OBEC Content Center** (contentcenter.obec.go.th): OBEC's digital media repository with 8 content types (e-book, application, video, audio, image, test, template, flash/multimedia). It is accessible on the web and as a phone app, for students, teachers, supervisors and the public. — [kroowit.in.th](https://www.kroowit.in.th/content-center); [OBEC Content Center guide (krukob)](https://krukob.com/web/occ-5/); [Maetuen School best practice](https://maetuen.ac.th/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Best-Practice-OBEC-Content-Center.pdf)
- **DLTV** (dltv.ac.th) is used as an on-demand channel alongside the OBEC Content Center, run with OBEC's Center for Teaching and Learning Technology (สทร.). — [krukob.com](https://krukob.com/web/media-4/)
- A policy critic warns the planned new platform would duplicate the OBEC Content Center, IPST's Project 14 and DLTV, and might not attract real use. — [kidforkids.org, Sep 2025](https://kidforkids.org/anywhere-anytime/)
- **Google Classroom** is used day to day for lessons and file sharing in at least some secondary schools. — [Thai PBS World, Nov 2024](https://www.thaipbsworld.com/education/free-tablet-device-scheme...-a-solution-or-another-problem%3F)
- **LINE**: about 56 million MAU in Thailand, roughly 85.7% of the online population (DataReportal, Jan 2025). LY Corp gives about 54 million. — [Relevant Audience](https://www.relevantaudience.com/digital-marketing-en/line-thailand-business-solutions-2026/); [LY Corp, Dec 2025](https://www.lycorp.co.jp/en/story/20251205/line_thailand.html)
- **LINE OpenChat**: 24 million Thai MAU (Nov 2026). Education is one of its most common uses: parents coordinate school notices, and high-school students share exam information. LINE says it is expanding partnerships with schools. — [Malay Mail / Media Outreach, 5 Nov 2026](https://www.malaymail.com/news/money/mediaoutreach/2026/11/05/line-openchat-introduces-new-community-discovery-experience-on-line-apps-third-tab-reaching-24-million-monthly-active-users-in-thailand/499242); [LY Corp, Dec 2025](https://www.lycorp.co.jp/en/story/20251223/openchat_thailand.html)
- Teachers historically relied on YouTube, MS Office and OBEC and IPST websites (depa 2016). — [hongpakkroo.com](https://www.hongpakkroo.com/2965.html)

### Inferences
- Distribution design:
  - Every activity should have a **short shareable URL and QR code** that opens directly in LINE's in-app browser and in Google Classroom without installation. LINE's in-app browser must be tested for WebGL and fullscreen.
  - Provide downloadable lesson packs (PDF worksheets plus a link) suited to upload to the OBEC Content Center, DEEP or a school Google Drive.
  - Offer **LTI or Google Classroom "share to Classroom"** buttons later.
- Seeking listing on the OBEC Content Center and DEEP, or an IPST endorsement, would strengthen the "practical use potential" claim. Approval processes were not researched.

### Gaps
- DEEP and OBEC Content Center usage statistics (registered or active users, 2024–2026) were not found.
- Data on Microsoft 365 use in OBEC schools and the account domain for students (e.g., @obec.moe.go.th or school domains) was not found.
- No survey quantifying Thai teachers' LINE use for teaching was found. Evidence is anecdotal or from small studies.
- DLTV's current reach (number of small schools receiving the satellite broadcast) was not verified in this session.

## 7. Summary: concrete design requirements derived from the constraints

### Takeaway
The Thai school context points to a lightweight, Thai-first and offline-capable design. It should have no student logins, work through phones and projectors, ship with complete Thai lesson kits, and degrade gracefully from 3D to 2D. Heavy 3D, English-only, account-based or always-online designs would fail "appropriate technology" and "practical use" tests in most OBEC schools.

### Cited Findings
- All evidence for these requirements is cited in Sections 1–6. Key anchors: 17 students per school computer and 1 in 3 principals reporting insufficient bandwidth ([ITU 2022](https://thailand.un.org/sites/default/files/2022-09/21-00630_E-learning-Thailand-Mapping-digital-divide%5B74%5D.pdf)); leased iPads, tablets and Chromebooks for M.4–6 only, rolled out slowly ([Daily News, Oct 2025](https://www.dailynews.co.th/news/5224071/); [Sangkha School, Feb 2026](https://sangkha.ac.th/4306/)); PDPA s.20 minors' consent ([pdpathailand.com](https://pdpathailand.com/pdpa/content_eng/article20_eng.php)); 74% of teachers lack an integration framework ([EdTech Hub 2025](https://edtechhub.org/2025/08/07/building-competencies-for-a-digital-age)); EF EPI "very low" English ([EF 2025](https://www.ef.com/assetscdn/WIBIwq6RdJvcD9bc8RMd/cefcom-epi-site/fact-sheets/2025/ef-epi-fact-sheet-thailand-english.pdf)).

### Inferences
Each requirement below is a design inference, and the driver column points to the evidence in the sections above.

| # | Requirement | Driver (evidence) |
|---|---|---|
| R1 | **Runs on a budget Android phone (~3–4 GB RAM, Mali-G52-class GPU, Android 8+), iPad Safari, Chromebook, and integrated-GPU Windows PCs**; target 30 fps with adaptive quality. | Smartphones are the main student device; mixed leased fleet (iPads, tablets, Chromebooks); budget-GPU limits (S1, S2) |
| R2 | **First interaction within a ≤3 MB download; usable on ≈1 Mbps per device**; progressive LOD streaming; total lesson ≤15–20 MB cached. | No school bandwidth data; 1 in 3 principals report insufficient bandwidth; ITU 0.5–1.4 Mbit/s per device benchmark (S1) |
| R3 | **Offline/PWA lesson packs**, with a service-worker cache so 30+ students do not each re-download; teacher can pre-load before class. | Unstable rural links; 6% of schools on 3G/4G; teachers paying for their own data (S1) |
| R4 | **No dependence on a sleeping back end**: static/CDN-hosted core content; API (Render free plan, ~1 min cold start) only for optional live NASA data, with cached snapshots as fallback. | Class-period timing; concurrency (S1 inference) |
| R5 | **2D/low-graphics fallback** (pre-rendered images or SVG) auto-selected when WebGL fails or performance is low; it doubles as the accessibility mode. | ~1–3.5% no or poor WebGL; old lab PCs (S2) |
| R6 | **Teacher-projector mode**: one device drives the whole class, with large labels and step-through narration. | ~17 students per computer; half of OBEC schools are small; lower-secondary students lack devices (S1) |
| R7 | **No student login required; no personal data collected**; class code and anonymous participation; teacher-side export only. | PDPA s.20: all secondary students are minors under 20, so parental consent is needed for consent-based processing (S5) |
| R8 | **Thai-first UI** with Royal Society (ราชบัณฑิตยสภา) astronomy terms, English/IAU names in brackets, optional EN toggle for EP/MEP; Thai fonts and line-breaking handled in 2D and 3D text. | EF EPI "very low" English proficiency; Thai national platforms (S4) |
| R9 | **Ready-to-teach Thai kit**: OBEC-format แผนการจัดการเรียนรู้, printable ใบงาน and ใบความรู้, answer keys, rubric, slides, mapped to the IPST earth and space indicators; each lesson fits 50 minutes. | 80% of teachers use tech for planning; 74% lack an integration framework; heavy workload (27.31 h/week in small schools) (S3) |
| R10 | **Printable worksheets work without devices** (unplugged and hybrid options), and activities include hands-on or offline steps. | Device gaps; screen-time concerns (S1, S5) |
| R11 | **Zero-install sharing**: short URL plus QR code, opens in the LINE in-app browser and Google Classroom; packs uploadable to DEEP, OBEC Content Center or school Drive. | LINE ~56M MAU; OpenChat education use; Classroom in use; existing national platforms (S6) |
| R12 | **WCAG 2.1 AA** (contrast, keyboard controls for 3D, alt text and audio description, captions, adjustable text size). | DGA government web standard references WCAG 2.1; WCAG 2.2 in newer guidelines (S5) |
| R13 | **Short teacher PD module** (1–2 h, video plus guide; potentially via IPST's online training system). | 8M THB training budget vs ~46 bn devices; lack of professional resources in disadvantaged schools (S3) |
| R14 | **Equity framing**: design for opportunity-expansion and small rural schools first, not only well-equipped "quality" schools. | 2025 rollout reached only 1,018 mostly well-equipped schools; urban–rural gaps in bandwidth and devices (S1) |

### Gaps
- The numeric targets (≤3 MB first load, ~1 Mbps per device, 30 fps on a Mali-G52-class GPU, ≤15–20 MB per lesson) are engineering judgements. No Thai standard specifies them, and they should be validated by field tests in 2–3 real schools (one urban "quality" school, one opportunity-expansion school, one small rural school) before the award submission.
- Device specs of the Anywhere Anytime fleet, current school bandwidth, and 2026 rollout status should be confirmed with OBEC or a partner ESA. These would be strong evidence for the proposal.
